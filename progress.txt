## Iteration 15 — REGEX-1: rule_engine.py Full Audit
**Task:** Read all 730 lines of `backend/app/services/rule_engine.py`. Document every regex pattern and rule. Classify each as KEEP/REPLACE/REMOVE.
**File:** `backend/app/services/rule_engine.py` (730 lines)

### File Structure
- **Lines 1-26**: Imports + logger
- **Lines 29-33**: `RiskLevel` enum (RED/YELLOW/GREEN)
- **Lines 36-51**: `RulePattern` dataclass (v1 rule format)
- **Lines 53-68**: `SmartRule` dataclass (v2, extends RulePattern with negative_patterns, escalation/de-escalation)
- **Lines 71-94**: `RuleMatch` dataclass (output of matching, includes cache_key for AI responses)
- **Lines 97-113**: Risk escalation/de-escalation helpers (`_escalate_risk`, `_deescalate_risk`)
- **Lines 120-312**: `DEFAULT_RULES` list — 13 built-in rules (not 16 as PRD states)
- **Lines 315-730**: `RuleEngine` class

### All 13 DEFAULT_RULES — Regex Patterns Inventory

**1. `unlimited_liability` (RED, deal_breaker=True)**
- `\b(unlimited\s+liability)\b`
- `\b(no\s+(cap|limit|limitation)\s+(on|to)\s+liability)\b`
- `\b(without\s+limit(ation)?\s+of\s+liability)\b`
- `\b(liability\s+shall\s+not\s+be\s+limited)\b`
- **Classification: REPLACE-WITH-AI** — These catch obvious phrases but miss nuanced unlimited liability clauses. E.g., "The Vendor's aggregate liability hereunder shall not be subject to any monetary limitation" would NOT match any pattern. AI understands semantic equivalents.

**2. `unilateral_termination` (RED, deal_breaker=True)**
- `\b(terminate\s+(this\s+)?(agreement|contract)\s+(at\s+)?any\s+time)\b`
- `\b(termination\s+without\s+(cause|reason))\b`
- `\b(may\s+terminate\s+(immediately|forthwith)\s+without\s+(notice|cause))\b`
- **Classification: REPLACE-WITH-AI** — Misses "Either party reserves the right to discontinue this arrangement at its sole discretion" — semantically identical but uses different vocabulary. Also can't distinguish buyer vs seller termination rights (context-dependent risk).

**3. `broad_indemnification` (SmartRule, base YELLOW, can escalate to RED)**
- Positive patterns (4): `indemnify and hold harmless`, `defend, indemnify, hold harmless`, `indemnif(y|ication) against any and all`, `unlimited indemnif(y|ication)`
- Negative patterns (1): `mutual(ly)?.{0,80}limited\s+to` — suppresses if mutual+limited
- Escalation (3): `one-sided|unilateral`, `uncapped|without cap|no cap`, `any and all (loss|damage|claim|liabilit)`
- De-escalation (1): `mutual(ly)?.{0,120}capped? (at|to)`
- **Classification: REPLACE-WITH-AI** — This is the most sophisticated rule (SmartRule with escalation logic), but still can't handle: (a) clauses that describe indemnification without using the word "indemnify", (b) cross-reference indemnification ("as set forth in Section 8.2"), (c) carve-outs that change the risk profile.

**4. `ip_assignment` (RED, deal_breaker=True)**
- `\b(work(s)?\s+for\s+hire)\b`
- `\b(assign(s|ment)?\s+(of\s+)?all\s+(intellectual\s+property|IP))\b`
- `\b(all\s+(rights|IP|intellectual\s+property)\s+shall\s+(belong|vest)\s+to)\b`
- **Classification: REPLACE-WITH-AI** — Misses "any developments, inventions, or improvements made during the term shall become the exclusive property of Client" — no "work for hire" or "assign" keyword.

**5. `auto_renewal` (YELLOW)**
- `\b(auto(matic(ally)?)?[\s-]?renew(al|s|ed)?)\b`
- `\b(shall\s+(automatically\s+)?renew\s+for\s+(successive|additional))\b`
- `\b(renew(s|ed)?\s+automatically)\b`
- **Classification: KEEP** — Auto-renewal uses very standard language. Regex is fast and accurate here. False negatives unlikely.

**6. `assignment_restriction` (YELLOW)**
- `\b(may\s+not\s+assign)\b`
- `\b(no\s+assignment\s+without\s+consent)\b`
- `\b(assignment\s+(is\s+)?(prohibited|restricted))\b`
- **Classification: REPLACE-WITH-AI** — Needs context: "may not assign without prior written consent, such consent not to be unreasonably withheld" is standard and fine. "may not assign under any circumstances" is problematic. Regex can't distinguish.

**7. `non_compete` (YELLOW)**
- `\b(non[\s-]?compete)\b`
- `\b(shall\s+not\s+compete)\b`
- `\b(restriction\s+on\s+competition)\b`
- **Classification: REPLACE-WITH-AI** — Needs contextual analysis of scope, duration, geography. "Non-compete limited to 6 months in Mumbai" vs "Non-compete worldwide for 5 years" are drastically different risks.

**8. `exclusive_dealing` (YELLOW)**
- `\b(exclusive\s+(right|license|agreement))\b`
- `\b(sole\s+and\s+exclusive)\b`
- `\b(exclusivity\s+(period|term|clause))\b`
- **Classification: REPLACE-WITH-AI** — "Exclusive right" could be perfectly fine (exclusive license to use software) or dangerous (exclusive dealing arrangement). Context-dependent.

**9. `confidentiality_term` (YELLOW)**
- `\b(perpetual\s+confidentiality)\b`
- `\b(confidential(ity)?\s+(in\s+)?perpetuity)\b`
- `\b(survive\s+(indefinitely|forever|in\s+perpetuity))\b`
- **Classification: KEEP as pre-filter** — The keyword "perpetual" or "perpetuity" is a strong signal. But AI should evaluate whether perpetual confidentiality is actually problematic in context (trade secrets vs general business info).

**10. `non_solicitation` (YELLOW)**
- `\b(non[\s-]?solicit(ation)?)\b`
- `\b(shall\s+not\s+solicit)\b`
- `solicit.{0,100}(employee|personnel|staff|contractor)`
- `\b(solicit,?\s+(induce,?\s+)?(recruit|hire|employ))\b`
- `\b(recruit(ing)?\s+(any\s+)?employee)\b`
- **Classification: KEEP as pre-filter** — Keyword-based detection is appropriate. AI should evaluate scope/duration.

**11. `reverse_engineering` (YELLOW)**
- `\b(reverse\s+engineer(ing)?)\b`
- `\b(decompil(e|ation)|disassembl(e|y))\b`
- `\b(derive\s+source\s+code)\b`
- **Classification: KEEP** — Very specific technical terms. Regex is perfect here.

**12. `governing_law` (GREEN)**
- `\b(govern(ed|ing)\s+(by|law))\b`
- `\b(laws\s+of\s+(the\s+)?(state|country|jurisdiction))\b`
- `\b(applicable\s+law)\b`
- **Classification: KEEP** — Standard clause detection. GREEN risk = informational only. Regex appropriate.

**13. `notice_provision` (GREEN)**
- `\b(notice(s)?\s+(shall|must|should)\s+be\s+(sent|given|provided))\b`
- `\b(written\s+notice)\b`
- **Classification: KEEP** — Standard clause detection. GREEN risk = informational only.

### RuleEngine Class Methods Analysis

**`__init__` (lines 328-390)** — KEEP
- Loads rules (custom, expanded 75-rule library, or default 13)
- Compiles all regex patterns at init time (good for performance)
- Handles SmartRule negative/escalation patterns
- Robust error handling for invalid regex

**`evaluate` (lines 392-513)** — KEEP (but role should change)
- Normalizes text via `normalize_text()` before matching
- For each rule: finds all regex matches, applies negative suppression, expands to sentence, deduplicates
- SmartRule: checks escalation/de-escalation in context window
- **Key insight**: This should become a PRE-FILTER that feeds candidates to AI, not a standalone risk assessor. Currently its output goes directly to results.

**`_remove_overlapping_matches` (lines 515-547)** — KEEP
- Deduplicates within same rule using 100-char proximity threshold
- Good heuristic for sentence-level dedup

**`_dedupe_cross_rule_matches` (lines 549-566)** — KEEP
- Prevents same text being flagged by multiple rules
- Uses normalized text comparison

**`_expand_to_sentence` (lines 568-608)** — KEEP
- Expands regex match to surrounding sentence (max 300 chars)
- Sentence boundary detection via `.!?;`
- **Issue**: `;` as sentence boundary is debatable in legal text where semicolons separate list items within a single clause. Could cause truncated context.

**`get_risk_summary` (lines 610-622)** — KEEP (utility)

**`get_default_rules` (lines 624-627)** — KEEP (utility)

**`from_playbook_rules` (lines 629-730)** — KEEP (but needs migration path)
- Converts PlaybookRule DB objects into RulePattern/SmartRule instances
- Supports 3 match_type modes: `exact` (escapes regex chars), `fuzzy` (word boundary), `regex` (raw)
- Validates regex at compile time, skips invalid patterns
- **Critical finding**: This is where user-created playbook rules become regex matchers. If we migrate to AI-native playbooks, this method needs the biggest change.
- **Security note** (line 663-664): Raw regex patterns from users are compiled directly. No ReDoS protection. A malicious user could craft a catastrophic backtracking regex like `(a+)+$` that hangs the server.

### Summary Classification

| Classification | Count | Rules |
|---|---|---|
| **KEEP** | 5 | auto_renewal, reverse_engineering, governing_law, notice_provision, confidentiality_term (as pre-filter) |
| **REPLACE-WITH-AI** | 8 | unlimited_liability, unilateral_termination, broad_indemnification, ip_assignment, assignment_restriction, non_compete, exclusive_dealing, non_solicitation (keep as pre-filter) |
| **REMOVE** | 0 | None — all rules serve a purpose, but 8 should transition to AI-primary with regex as optional pre-filter |

### Critical Findings

1. **ReDoS vulnerability** (line 663-664): `from_playbook_rules()` compiles user-supplied regex patterns without ReDoS protection. A malicious regex could cause CPU exhaustion. This is a P0 security issue.

2. **Semicolon as sentence boundary** (line 588): Legal contracts use semicolons within clauses for list items. Using `;` as a sentence terminator truncates clause context, leading to incomplete match text being sent to AI for verification.

3. **13 rules, not 16**: The PRD mentions "16 built-in rules" but DEFAULT_RULES has only 13. The expanded library (rules_library.py, 75 rules) is the actual default.

4. **SmartRule escalation is order-dependent** (lines 463-490): De-escalation is checked FIRST and takes priority. If both escalation and de-escalation patterns match, de-escalation wins. This is intentional but not documented for users.

5. **from_playbook_rules uses rule.clause_type as the name** (line 705): `name=rule.clause_type` — this means the rule name shown to users is the clause_type, not the rule's actual title. Could cause confusing UI labels.

6. **Normalized text dedup is too aggressive** (lines 556-566): If two genuinely different clauses have the same normalized text (e.g., two "governing law" clauses in different sections), only the first is kept. This is a false-negative risk for contracts with repeated standard language.

## Iteration 17 — REGEX-3: clause_classifier.py Full Audit (968 lines)

### Architecture Overview
The clause classifier is a **separate system from the rule engine**. While rule_engine.py detects RISKS, clause_classifier.py classifies CLAUSE TYPES (is this an indemnification clause, a governing law clause, etc.). It's Stage 2 of the pipeline.

**Key design**: Two-tier pattern matching:
1. **Heading patterns** — match section headings (strong signal, 0.6 base score)
2. **Body patterns** — match clause body text (0.2 each, max 0.6)
3. **Weight multiplier** — per-rule priority (1.0-1.3)
4. **AI fallback** — clauses with confidence < 0.5 are flagged `needs_ai_review=True`

### Taxonomy: 30 Clause Types in 8 Groups + UNKNOWN
- Formation (5): definitions, recitals, entire_agreement, amendments, severability
- Term (6): duration, auto_renewal, termination_for_cause, termination_for_convenience, cure_period, survival
- Financial (5): payment_terms, late_payment, price_escalation, taxes, audit_rights
- Liability (4): liability_cap, consequential_damages, indemnification_scope, insurance
- IP (3): ip_ownership, license_grant, ip_indemnification
- Confidentiality (4): confidentiality_obligations, confidentiality_exceptions, data_protection, breach_notification
- Governance (4): governing_law, jurisdiction, arbitration, force_majeure
- Restrictive (3): non_compete, non_solicitation, exclusivity
- UNKNOWN (1): fallback

**Actual count: 34 types** (30 named + UNKNOWN), not exactly "30" as the docstring claims.
**Classifier rules: 30** `_ClassifierRule` instances (one per type except UNKNOWN).

### Classification: KEEP (with enhancements)
**This classifier should MOSTLY be KEPT.** Here's why:

1. **It classifies, not assesses risk.** "Is this an indemnification clause?" is a categorization task where keyword matching is fast and effective. This is fundamentally different from "Is this indemnification clause risky?"

2. **The AI fallback is well-designed.** Ambiguous clauses (confidence < 0.5) are already flagged for AI review. This is the right architecture.

3. **Section splitting is structural** — `_split_into_sections()` (lines 876-963) uses numbered headings, ALL CAPS, and article markers. This is the correct approach; AI would be overkill for section splitting.

### Critical Findings

**1. Scoring ceiling creates false ambiguity (lines 835-853)**
- Heading match: 0.6
- Body matches: 0.2 each, capped at 0.6
- Max possible: 0.6 + 0.6 = 1.2, but confidence capped at 1.0 (line 872)
- With weights (up to 1.3): max = 1.2 × 1.3 = 1.56, capped to 1.0
- **Issue**: A clause with NO heading (common in contracts) starts at 0.0 heading score. Maximum from body alone = 0.6 × weight. For weight=1.0, body-only max is 0.6 — meaning body-only classification is ALWAYS flagged as ambiguous if < 0.5 threshold needs 3 body matches. This means contracts with inline clauses (no headings) will flood the AI with "ambiguous" classifications, wasting tokens.

**2. `_split_into_sections` heading detection too rigid (lines 886-902)**
- Only detects: numbered (`1.`, `2.1`), ARTICLE/SECTION N, SCHEDULE A, Roman numerals, ALL CAPS
- Misses: Bold text (common in Word documents — but text extraction loses formatting), "Part X", "Appendix", "Addendum"
- **Indian contracts**: Often use "(a)", "(b)", "(i)", "(ii)" sub-numbering which won't be detected as headings
- ALL CAPS detection requires minimum 6 chars (line 929: `len(stripped) > 5`) — misses short headings like "SLA" or "TERM"

**3. Taxonomy gaps — 30 types vs 78 rules**
The classifier has 30 types, but the rules library has 78 rules. Missing clause types in classifier:
- No SaaS/Technology types (SLA, data portability, security standards, API rights)
- No compliance types (anti-bribery, sanctions, regulatory)
- No assignment/change of control types
- No transition assistance type
- No insolvency/bankruptcy type
- This means ~20+ rules from rules_library.py detect clauses that the classifier can't even categorize

**4. `UNKNOWN` maps to `ClauseGroup.FORMATION` (line 128)**
When a clause can't be classified, its group defaults to FORMATION. This is misleading — an unclassifiable clause about data breach notifications would appear under "Formation" in the UI.

**5. No de-duplication of section splits**
If a contract has "1. DEFINITIONS" followed by "1.1 Defined Terms", both are detected as separate sections with separate headings. The sub-section gets classified independently, potentially splitting a single logical clause into fragments.

**6. Singleton at module level (line 967)**
`clause_classifier = ClauseClassifier()` — compiled at import time. Good for performance (regex compiled once), but means rules can't be customized per-tenant without creating new instances.

### Pattern Quality Assessment
- **Heading patterns**: Generally well-crafted with appropriate `\b` boundaries
- **Body patterns**: More complex regex with lookaheads; good coverage of legal phrasing
- **Smart quote handling** (line 214): `["\u201c]\w+["\u201d]` — correctly handles Unicode smart quotes in definitions. Good.
- **Negative lookahead** (line 282): `\bterm\b(?!\s+(?:sheet|inati))` — prevents "termination" or "term sheet" from matching "Duration/term" heading. Smart.

### Verdict: KEEP but enhance
- **KEEP**: Entire classifier architecture, heading/body pattern approach, AI fallback
- **ENHANCE**: Add missing clause types (SaaS, compliance, assignment), fix UNKNOWN group mapping, improve section splitting for Indian contracts
- **REPLACE**: Nothing — clause classification is a categorization task well-suited to keyword matching

## Iteration 18 — REGEX-4: scope_analyzer.py Full Audit (451 lines)
**Task:** Read all 451 lines (not 406 as PRD states) of `backend/app/services/scope_analyzer.py`.
**File:** `backend/app/services/scope_analyzer.py` (451 lines)

### Architecture Overview
ScopeAnalyzer runs BETWEEN Stage 2 (rule engine) and Stage 3 (AI). It takes RuleMatch objects and analyzes each clause's **scope dimensions** using regex:
1. **Breadth** — narrow/standard/broad/unlimited
2. **Mutuality** — mutual/one-sided favorable/one-sided unfavorable/unclear
3. **Financial Exposure** — capped/uncapped/not applicable
4. **Duration** — fixed term/perpetual/survival/not specified
5. **Trigger** — material breach/any breach/negligence/gross negligence/willful misconduct

Each dimension produces a sub-score, and a weighted composite `scope_score` (1-10) determines `risk_adjustment` (-1, 0, or +1).

### Classification: REPLACE-WITH-AI (mostly)

**This is exactly the kind of analysis AI does better.** Scope analysis requires understanding SEMANTIC CONTEXT:

1. **Breadth detection fails on paraphrasing**: "any and all" is caught, but "the broadest extent permitted by law" is not. "Without limitation" is caught, but "there shall be no restriction on the types of claims covered" is not.

2. **Mutuality detection is too simplistic (lines 302-313)**:
   - Detects "each party" or "both parties" as mutual ✓
   - But MISSES asymmetric obligations phrased as separate sentences: "The Vendor shall indemnify the Client..." in one paragraph, with no corresponding "The Client shall indemnify the Vendor..." — this is one-sided, but the analyzer would mark it UNCLEAR since neither mutual nor one-sided patterns match.
   - **Critical flaw**: `ONE_SIDED_INDICATORS` (lines 166-171) hardcode party names: "company", "vendor", "provider", "licensor", "employer", "customer", "client", "licensee", "employee". What about "Seller", "Buyer", "Supplier", "Consultant", "Contractor", "Subscriber", "End User", "Recipient", "Disclosing Party"? These common terms are completely missed.

3. **Financial exposure cap extraction (lines 174-177)**:
   - Pattern captures dollar amounts and "months of fees" phrasing
   - **Good**: Handles multiple currencies (USD, INR, GBP, EUR, £, $, ₹)
   - **Good**: Handles Indian numbering (lakh, crore)
   - **Miss**: "aggregate liability shall not exceed the fees paid in the preceding twelve (12) month period" — the word "twelve" written out (not digit) won't match `\d+\s*months`
   - **Miss**: Percentage-based caps ("100% of fees paid under this SOW") won't match

4. **Duration detection has priority bug (lines 330-351)**:
   - Checks perpetual FIRST, survival SECOND, fixed-term THIRD
   - If a clause says "shall survive termination for a period of 3 years", it matches SURVIVAL (correct)
   - But if it says "this irrevocable license shall terminate after 5 years", "irrevocable" triggers PERPETUAL even though there's a fixed 5-year term
   - `irrevocable` ≠ `perpetual` in legal terms. An irrevocable license can still have a fixed term.

5. **Trigger detection conflates gross negligence and willful misconduct (line 198)**:
   `TriggerType.GROSS_NEGLIGENCE` patterns include `willful\s+misconduct` — this means gross negligence and willful misconduct are treated as equivalent triggers. But `TriggerType.WILLFUL_MISCONDUCT` also exists separately (line 199). Due to the check order in `_detect_trigger` (lines 356-361), WILLFUL_MISCONDUCT is checked first, so the duplicate in GROSS_NEGLIGENCE is dead code but confusing.

### Scoring Model Analysis (lines 363-404)

Weights: Breadth 25%, Mutuality 25%, Financial 25%, Duration 15%, Trigger 10%

**Risk adjustment thresholds**:
- scope_score >= 7.5 → escalate (+1)
- scope_score <= 3.0 → de-escalate (-1)
- Otherwise → no adjustment (0)

**Issue**: The 7.5 escalation threshold is quite high. Let's check worst case:
- Unlimited breadth (10.0 × 0.25 = 2.5)
- One-sided unfavorable (9.0 × 0.25 = 2.25)
- Uncapped exposure (9.0 × 0.25 = 2.25)
- Perpetual (9.0 × 0.15 = 1.35)
- Any breach (8.0 × 0.10 = 0.80)
- **Worst case: 9.15** → correctly escalates

Best case: Narrow(2×.25) + Mutual(3×.25) + Capped(3×.25) + Fixed(3×.15) + Willful(3×.10) = 0.5+0.75+0.75+0.45+0.3 = **2.75** → correctly de-escalates

**Middle ground concern**: A clause that is BROAD (7.5×.25=1.875) + ONE_SIDED_UNFAVORABLE (9×.25=2.25) + NOT_APPLICABLE financial (5×.25=1.25) + NOT_SPECIFIED duration (5×.15=0.75) + NONE_SPECIFIED trigger (5×.10=0.50) = **6.625** → NO adjustment. This seems too lenient — a broad, one-sided clause should probably escalate.

- **Issue**: The `ALL_CLAUSE_TYPES` list (lines 94-129) uses different IDs than the rules in rules_library.py. E.g., scope_analyzer uses "confidentiality" but rules_library uses "confidentiality_obligations". "assignment" vs "assignment_restriction". "sla" vs "sla_terms". This means coverage calculation may be inaccurate — types detected by rules won't match the strings in ALL_CLAUSE_TYPES.

### Critical Findings Summary

1. **Party name hardcoding** (lines 166-171): ONE_SIDED_INDICATORS only catch 5 party labels each for buyer/seller. Real contracts use dozens of different party designations. This causes most one-sided clauses to be classified as UNCLEAR.

2. **`irrevocable` ≠ `perpetual`** (line 183): False perpetual detection for irrevocable licenses with fixed terms.

3. **Clause type ID mismatch**: ALL_CLAUSE_TYPES uses different IDs than rules_library.py, causing inaccurate coverage metrics.

4. **Written-out numbers not captured**: "twelve months" won't match the `\d+\s*months` pattern.

## Iteration 19 — REGEX-5: defined_terms_resolver.py Full Audit (456 lines)
**Task:** Read all 456 lines of `backend/app/services/defined_terms_resolver.py`. Document defined term extraction.
**File:** `backend/app/services/defined_terms_resolver.py` (456 lines, not 434 as PRD states)

### Architecture Overview
4-step pipeline:
1. **Extract** — 7 regex patterns match "Term" means/shall mean/is defined as/refers to/hereinafter/colon-separated definitions
2. **Cross-reference** — Finds which definitions reference other defined terms
3. **Resolve** — Recursively expands cross-references up to depth 3
4. **Flag overbroad** — 9 regex patterns detect dangerously broad definition language

Output feeds into AI prompts via `to_prompt_context()` — this gives the AI visibility into what "Confidential Information" or "Intellectual Property" ACTUALLY means in this specific contract.

### Classification: KEEP

**This is an ideal regex use case.** Defined terms follow highly predictable syntactic patterns:
- `"Capitalized Term" means ...`
- `"Capitalized Term" shall mean ...`
- `(hereinafter "Term")`

Regex is FASTER, MORE RELIABLE, and CHEAPER than AI for this extraction. The output feeds into AI prompts, which is the correct architecture.

### Detailed Pattern Analysis

**7 Definition Extraction Patterns (lines 108-158):**

1. `"Term" means/shall mean/will mean ...` — Core pattern, handles most definitions ✓
2. `"Term" is/are defined as ...` — Alternative definition syntax ✓
3. `As used herein, "Term" means/refers to ...` — Inline definition ✓
4. `"Term" has/shall have the meaning ...` — Cross-reference style ✓
5. `"Term" refers to ...` — Simple reference ✓
6. `(hereinafter "Term")` — Parenthetical definition ✓ — **Note**: Groups are REVERSED (definition first, term second). Code handles this correctly (lines 269-274).
7. `"Term": definition` — Colon/em-dash separated ✓

**All patterns handle smart quotes** (`\u201c`, `\u201d`) ✓

**Definition boundary detection**: Uses lookahead `(?=\n["\u201c][A-Z]|\n\n|\n\d+\.\s|\Z)` — stops at:
- Next quoted capitalized term on new line
- Double newline (paragraph break)
- Numbered section heading
- End of text

### Critical Findings

**1. Definition boundary fails for inline definitions (all patterns)**
The lookahead `\n\n|\n\d+\.\s` assumes definitions are paragraph-separated. But many contracts have INLINE definitions:
> `"Confidential Information" means all information disclosed by either party, whether written or oral, and "Receiving Party" means the party receiving such information.`
In this case, the regex would capture EVERYTHING after "Confidential Information means" all the way to the end of the paragraph, including the definition of "Receiving Party". The second term's definition would then be a subset of the first.

**2. Term name length limit of 80 chars (line 111)**
Pattern: `[A-Z][A-Za-z\s&,/()-]{1,80}?`. Indian contracts sometimes have long defined terms:
- `"Minimum Guaranteed Annual Revenue Commitment"` (46 chars) ✓
- `"Intellectual Property Rights and Proprietary Information of the Disclosing Party"` (82 chars) ✗ — would be truncated

**3. Parenthetical pattern (Pattern 6) captures too much context (line 147)**
Pattern: `([A-Za-z][^.]{10,200}?)` captures 10-200 chars BEFORE the parenthetical as the "definition". This could match half a sentence that isn't really a definition:
> `The parties agree that all software code developed during the engagement (hereinafter "Deliverables") shall be...`
Captured "definition" would be: `The parties agree that all software code developed during the engagement` — this is context, not a precise definition.

**4. Colon pattern (Pattern 7) has no uppercase restriction on right side (line 154)**
Could match section headings like `"DEFINITIONS": This section defines the terms...` where the text after the colon is not actually a definition of the term "DEFINITIONS".

**5. Overbroad detection is well-calibrated (lines 161-198)**
9 signals with a 2-signal threshold for flagging (or 1 high-severity signal). Covers:
- "all information", "any and all", "without limitation"
- "whether or not marked", "including but not limited to"
- "in any form or medium", "directly or indirectly"
- "all intellectual property", "everything/totality"
**Assessment**: These are exactly the right phrases that a corporate lawyer would flag. Well-done.

**6. Recursive resolution has circular reference protection (line 406)**
`visited` set prevents infinite loops. MAX_RECURSION_DEPTH=3 prevents runaway expansion. Both are correct safeguards.

**7. `to_prompt_context()` truncates definitions at 400 chars (line 63)**
For a contract with 50 defined terms averaging 200 chars each, the prompt context would be ~10,000 chars (~2,500 tokens). This is a meaningful chunk of the AI's context window but within budget.

**8. Missing pattern: Bold/underlined defined terms**
Many Word documents use **bold** or __underline__ to indicate defined terms without quotes. Since text extraction strips formatting, these terms would be invisible to the resolver. This is a limitation of the text extraction layer, not the resolver itself.

### Verdict: KEEP
- **KEEP**: All 7 extraction patterns, cross-reference resolution, overbroad detection
- **ENHANCE**: Fix inline definition boundary, increase term name length limit
- **REPLACE**: Nothing — defined term extraction is a textbook regex use case
- **Integration with AI is correct**: Resolver output feeds into AI prompts via `to_prompt_context()`, giving AI the definitions it needs for contextual analysis

## Iteration 22 — REGEX-8: confidence_scorer.py Full Audit (339 lines)
**Task:** Read all 339 lines (not "250+" as PRD states) of `backend/app/services/confidence_scorer.py`. Document the scoring formula and the regex+AI bias.
**File:** `backend/app/services/confidence_scorer.py` (339 lines)

### Architecture Overview
5-factor weighted scoring model for every AI-generated redline:

| Factor | Weight | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Text Verification | 30% | HallucinationGuard match confidence |
| Rule Corroboration | 25% | Did regex AND AI both flag this? |
| Playbook Alignment | 20% | How specific is the triggering playbook rule? |
| Model Confidence | 15% | AI's self-reported certainty |
| Cross-References | 10% | Do related rules corroborate? |

**Output levels**: HIGH (≥0.75), MEDIUM (0.50-0.74), LOW (<0.50)

### The Regex+AI Bias — Detailed Analysis

**Factor 2: Rule Corroboration (lines 238-254)** — This is the core of the PRD's concern:

```
regex + AI agree  → 1.0
AI only           → 0.5
regex only        → 0.6
neither           → 0.0
```

**The bias**: AI-only findings get 0.5 (50%), while regex+AI gets 1.0. This means:
- **Impact**: The 25% weight on this factor means AI-only findings lose 0.25 × 0.5 = **0.125 points** (12.5%) compared to regex+AI findings.
- **Real example**: An AI finding a novel risk not in any regex pattern starts with a 0.125 penalty. If text verification is high (0.99) and model confidence is high (0.85), the total score would be: 0.30×0.99 + 0.25×0.5 + 0.20×0.3 + 0.15×0.85 + 0.10×0.3 = 0.297 + 0.125 + 0.06 + 0.1275 + 0.03 = **0.6395** → MEDIUM.
- **With regex corroboration**, same finding: 0.297 + 0.25 + 0.06 + 0.1275 + 0.03 = **0.7645** → HIGH.
- **The gap: 0.125 points separates MEDIUM from HIGH** for identical AI quality.

**Is this bias justified?**
- **Argument FOR**: Regex corroboration is a positive signal. If both systems agree, confidence should be higher.
- **Argument AGAINST**: In an AI-first system, the AI may detect risks that NO regex covers (e.g., a novel force majeure carve-out, an unusual IP provision). Penalizing AI-only findings means novel, legitimate risks get lower visibility in the UI (collapsed by default at LOW, shown with verification badge at MEDIUM).
- **Recommendation**: Change AI-only from 0.5 to 0.7. The corroboration bonus should be a BOOST for regex+AI agreement, not a PENALTY for AI-only findings.

### Other Factor Analysis

**Factor 1: Text Verification (lines 228-236)**
Direct pass-through of HallucinationGuard confidence. Clean and correct. This is the most important factor at 30% weight — ensures the AI's quoted text actually exists in the contract.

**Factor 3: Playbook Alignment (lines 256-287)**
Scoring logic:
- No playbook rule → 0.3 (baseline)
- Has primary_position (>10 chars) → +0.3
- Has fallback_position (>10 chars) → +0.15
- Is deal_breaker → +0.1
- Has verification_prompt (>10 chars) → +0.15
- **Maximum: 1.0** (0.3 + 0.3 + 0.15 + 0.1 + 0.15)

**Issue**: The `len(str(primary)) > 10` check is too low. A primary_position of "Fix this" (8 chars) wouldn't count, but "Fix this now" (12 chars) would. Both are equally vague. The threshold should check for QUALITY, not just LENGTH. But length is the best heuristic available without AI.

**Factor 4: Model Confidence (lines 289-298)**
AI self-reported confidence. Default is 0.4 (conservative) when AI doesn't report. **Issue**: Does Gemini actually return a confidence score? If not, this factor is always 0.4, meaning 15% of the score is a constant. Need to verify in gemini_analyzer.py.

**Factor 5: Cross-References (lines 300-338)**
Hardcoded CORROBORATION_MAP with 9 rule pairs. Each corroborating rule adds 0.25.
**Issues**:
1. Only 9 of 78 rules have corroboration pairs defined. The other 69 rules always get the 0.3 baseline.
2. Rule names must match EXACTLY: "Unlimited Liability" in the map vs whatever `related_rule_names` contains. If the pipeline passes rule IDs instead of names, this never matches.
3. The map is asymmetric: "Perpetual Confidentiality" corroborates with "Non-Solicitation Clause", but "Non-Solicitation Clause" doesn't list "Perpetual Confidentiality" as a corroborator.

### Proposed New Scoring Model

For an AI-first system, the weights should shift:

| Factor | Current | Proposed | Rationale |
|---|---|---|---|
| Text Verification | 30% | 35% | Most important — does the quote exist? |
| Rule Corroboration | 25% | 15% | Bonus, not primary signal |
| Playbook Alignment | 20% | 15% | Playbook migration may change this |
| Model Confidence | 15% | 20% | AI is primary analyzer |
| Cross-References | 10% | 15% | Contextual corroboration is valuable |

And for Rule Corroboration scores:
```
regex + AI agree  → 1.0  (unchanged)
AI only           → 0.7  (up from 0.5)
regex only        → 0.5  (down from 0.6 — regex without AI should be suspicious)
neither           → 0.0  (unchanged)
```

### Classification: KEEP (with rebalancing)
The 5-factor model architecture is sound. The weights and individual factor scores need rebalancing for an AI-first system, but the framework should be preserved.

### Critical Findings Summary
1. **AI-only penalty of 0.125 points** (12.5%) can push legitimate novel findings from HIGH to MEDIUM visibility
2. **Model confidence factor may be constant 0.4** if Gemini doesn't return confidence scores
3. **Cross-reference map covers only 9/78 rules** and uses exact string matching
4. **Asymmetric corroboration pairs** — relationship isn't bidirectional
5. **Weight auto-normalization** (lines 106-109) is a good safety measure

## Iteration 23 — REGEX-9: hallucination_guard.py Full Audit (423 lines)
**Task:** Read all 423 lines (not "370" as PRD states). Document verification cascade, fuzzy threshold, deal-breaker handling.
**File:** `backend/app/services/hallucination_guard.py` (423 lines)

### Architecture Overview
4-tier verification cascade for every AI-generated quote:

| Tier | Method | Confidence | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Exact substring match | 1.0 | "exact" |
| 2 | Normalized match (case+whitespace) | 0.99 | "normalized" |
| 3 | Fuzzy match (rapidfuzz ≥80%) | score×0.95 | "fuzzy_corrected" |
| 4 | No match (<70%) | 0.0 | "rejected" |

Pre-computation on init: normalizes full contract text, generates 300-char sliding window segments with 100-char overlap for fuzzy matching.

### Critical Findings

**1. The 0.80 fuzzy threshold — is it correct? (line 127)**
`FUZZY_PASS_THRESHOLD = 0.80` means any AI quote that is 80%+ similar to some 300-char segment of the contract is accepted.

**Analysis**: 80% Levenshtein ratio on a 300-char segment means up to 60 characters can differ. For a legal clause like "The Vendor shall indemnify and hold harmless the Client", an 80% match could accept "The Vendor shall indemnify and hold harmless the Customer" (only "Client"→"Customer" changed). This is a DIFFERENT PARTY — the AI hallucinated the party name.

**However**: The fuzzy match uses `partial_ratio` (line 330), not `ratio`. `partial_ratio` finds the best matching substring, so a short AI quote matching a longer segment at 80%+ is more lenient than 80% of the full segment. For short quotes, the guard switches to `fuzz.ratio` with score_cutoff=85 (line 217), which is stricter.

**Recommendation**: The 0.80 threshold is on the edge. For legal text where a single word change can reverse meaning ("shall" → "shall not"), 0.85 would be safer. But increasing it risks rejecting valid quotes where AI adds/removes minor whitespace or punctuation.

**2. Deal-breaker rules at confidence 0.3 — PRD concern**
The PRD asks if deal-breaker rules are "kept at confidence 0.3". Looking at the code:
- `verify_quote()` never uses 0.3 as a threshold
- Fuzzy match confidence is `score/100 * 0.95` (line 225): an 80% fuzzy match gets confidence 0.76 (0.80 × 0.95)
- Rejected quotes get confidence 0.0
- The 0.3 appears nowhere in hallucination_guard.py

**Where does 0.3 come from?** It's in confidence_scorer.py: the *playbook_alignment* factor defaults to 0.3 for generic findings (no specific playbook rule). This is a different system. The hallucination guard itself does NOT keep deal-breakers at 0.3.

**3. `_find_actual_text()` position mapping is fragile (lines 358-414)**
This function reverse-maps normalized positions back to original text positions. The algorithm:
- Walks through original characters one by one
- Tracks "normalized position" by simulating normalization
- **Bug**: Line 386 checks `char in '"\' '` but the normalization function (`_normalize_for_comparison`) only strips quotes at EDGES, not internally. The position tracking doesn't account for:
  - Punctuation stripping at edges (`.strip('"\' .,;:')`)
  - Unicode normalization differences
  - The actual `_normalize_for_comparison` removes characters the walker doesn't
- **Result**: For quotes where normalization changes character count (e.g., stripping leading quotes), the returned original text may be offset by a few characters — extracting the wrong portion of the contract.

**4. Sliding window parameters are hardcoded (line 151)**
`window_size=300, step=100` — means segments overlap by 200 chars. For a 50-page contract (~100,000 chars), this generates ~1,000 segments. `process.extractOne` runs fuzzy comparison against ALL segments.

**Performance**: rapidfuzz is C-optimized and fast, but 1,000 segments × multiple quotes per analysis could be measurable. For a contract with 20 AI findings, that's 20 × 1,000 = 20,000 fuzzy comparisons. Still fast with rapidfuzz but worth monitoring.

**5. Token sort ratio fallback (lines 342-348)**
If `partial_ratio` fails, the guard falls back to `token_sort_ratio`, which sorts words alphabetically before comparing. This handles cases where AI reorders words in a quote. **Risk**: Token sort can produce false positives — "The Client shall not indemnify the Vendor" and "The Vendor shall not indemnify the Client" have the same tokens and would match at 100%.

**6. Re-query mechanism is well-designed (lines 279-308)**
`needs_requery()` only triggers for deal-breaker rules that failed or had weak fuzzy matches (<85%). `get_requery_instruction()` generates a clear "copy verbatim" prompt. However, this function was identified as "disconnected" in iteration 9 (AUDIT-9) — it exists but may not be called by the pipeline.

**7. Empty quote handling (lines 170-180)**
Empty/blank quotes are immediately rejected with confidence 0.0. Correct behavior — AI sometimes returns empty original_text fields.

**8. `_normalize_for_comparison` strips semantic punctuation (line 80)**
Stripping `,;:` from edges means "Section 8:" and "Section 8" are treated identically. Fine for matching. But the confidence of 0.99 for normalized matches may overstate confidence when the normalization was actually aggressive.

### Verdict: KEEP (with threshold refinement)
The hallucination guard is a critical safety layer. The 4-tier verification cascade is well-architected. The main concerns are:
- 0.80 fuzzy threshold may be too lenient for legal text where single-word changes reverse meaning
- `_find_actual_text()` position mapping has offset bugs
- Token sort fallback can produce false positives with word reordering
- Re-query mechanism exists but may be disconnected from pipeline

### Classification: KEEP — This is the most important quality gate in the system

## Iteration 27 — PLAYBOOK-3: playbook_cache.py + playbook_conditions_engine.py Audit
**Files:** `playbook_cache.py` (158 lines), `playbook_conditions_engine.py` (650 lines)

### playbook_cache.py (158 lines)
Simple in-memory LRU-style cache for RuleEngine instances and rule dicts.

**Architecture:**
- `_engine_cache`: (playbook_id, updated_at) → RuleEngine — avoids re-compiling regex
- `_rules_dict_cache`: (playbook_id, updated_at) → list of rule dicts — for prompt injection
- `_MAX_CACHE_SIZE = 64` — FIFO eviction when full
- `invalidate_playbook_cache()` — called on update/delete from endpoints

**Key function**: `load_playbook()` (lines 108-157) — async DB loader with eager-loading of rules_list, access control checks. This is the primary entry point for the analysis pipeline.

**Findings:**
1. **FIFO eviction is not LRU** (line 55): `_engine_cache.pop(next(iter(_engine_cache)))` removes the OLDEST entry, not the least recently USED. A frequently-used playbook could be evicted if 64 other playbooks are loaded. For production with many orgs, this could cause cache thrashing.
2. **Cache key uses `updated_at.isoformat()`** (line 45): If `updated_at` has different microsecond precision between queries, the key won't match and cache is useless. Unlikely with PostgreSQL but possible with different DB backends.
3. **No thread safety**: Module-level dicts without locks. With async concurrency, two coroutines could simultaneously try to evict+insert, potentially corrupting the dict. In practice, Python's GIL prevents this for dict operations, but it's not guaranteed for async scenarios.

### playbook_conditions_engine.py (650 lines)
Sophisticated conditional logic engine — the most well-engineered service file in the codebase.

**Architecture:**
- `DealContext` dataclass: counterparty_type, deal_size, jurisdiction, contract_side, custom_fields
- `ConditionsEngine` with 3-step pipeline:
  1. `evaluate_conditions()` — filter conditions that match deal context
  2. `apply_overrides()` — mutate/suppress RuleMatch objects based on overrides
  3. `process()` — async full pipeline (load from DB → evaluate → apply)

**Condition evaluators:**
- String fields: equals, not_equals, in, not_in, contains
- Numeric fields: equals, not_equals, greater_than, less_than, between
- Custom fields: equals, not_equals, contains, in, not_in

**Findings:**
1. **This is entirely deterministic — no regex, no AI** — it's pure conditional logic based on structured metadata. Classification: KEEP.
2. **`apply_overrides` uses `copy.copy()` not `copy.deepcopy()`** (line 208): Shallow copy means nested mutable objects (like `analysis_notes` list in RuleMatch) are shared between original and copy. If the override adds to `analysis_notes`, it modifies the original too.
3. **Excellent logging**: Every condition evaluation, override application, and suppression is logged at INFO/DEBUG level. This is important for audit trails in legal software.
4. **Deal size comparison uses `==` for floats** (line 498): `context_value == threshold` for floating-point comparison is unreliable. Should use approximate comparison for deal sizes.
5. **Priority ordering is correct**: Higher priority conditions are evaluated first and their overrides applied first. If two conditions match and both override the same rule, the higher-priority one wins because it's applied first and subsequent overrides overwrite it. Wait — actually it's the OPPOSITE. Since both are applied sequentially, the LOWER priority condition's override overwrites the higher priority one. **This is a bug** — the last override wins, but it should be the highest-priority one.
6. **Well-documented**: Extensive docstrings, clear type hints, defensive error handling. This is production-quality code.

## Iteration 28 — PLAYBOOK-4: playbook_versioning.py Audit (650 lines)
**Task:** Read all 650 lines. Document versioning system and backward compatibility for schema migration.
**File:** `backend/app/services/playbook_versioning.py` (650 lines)

### Architecture
Full version control system with 4 operations:
1. **create_snapshot** — Serializes complete playbook state (rules+tiers+conditions+overrides+dependencies) to JSONB
2. **get_versions / get_version** — Retrieve version history
3. **diff_versions** — Field-level diff between two snapshots (rules added/removed/modified, conditions, dependencies)
4. **rollback** — Full state restoration from snapshot, preserving original UUIDs

### Schema Migration Implications
**If we migrate from regex-based `detection_patterns` to AI-native fields:**

1. **Snapshots store `detection_patterns` as-is** (line 82): `_serialize_rule` captures the full JSONB, so old snapshots will always contain the old schema. This is correct — snapshots are immutable history.

2. **Rollback recreates from snapshot** (lines 525-543): Rollback uses `rule_data.get("detection_patterns")` directly, so old snapshots with regex patterns will restore correctly. New snapshots with AI-native fields will also work because the rollback uses `.get()` with defaults for all new fields.

3. **Diff compares field values** (lines 149-184): `_diff_rule` compares all fields including `detection_patterns`. If the schema changes shape (from `{"patterns": [...]}` to `{"risk_description": "..."}`), the diff will show both as changed, which is correct behavior.

4. **Backward compatibility is good**: The versioning system is schema-agnostic — it serializes whatever fields exist and restores them. Adding new fields to PlaybookRule won't break existing snapshots because `_serialize_rule` captures all fields, and rollback uses `.get()` with defaults.

### Critical Findings

1. **Rollback is a destructive operation without undo** (lines 424-642): The rollback deletes ALL current rules, conditions, dependencies, then recreates from snapshot. If the rollback fails midway (e.g., DB error after deletes but before inserts), the playbook is left EMPTY. The entire operation should be wrapped in a savepoint/nested transaction.
   - Actually, it IS in a transaction (line 634: `await db.commit()`). If any step fails before commit, the transaction rolls back. But `db.commit()` is a single point of no return — the delete+insert is atomic. **This is actually correct.**

2. **Snapshot size concern**: A playbook with 200 rules, each with 4 tiers, plus 50 conditions with overrides, serialized as JSONB, could be 100KB+ per snapshot. With 100 versions, that's 10MB+ per playbook just for version history. No retention policy exists to prune old versions.

3. **Original UUIDs are preserved on rollback** (line 522): `new_rule_id = UUID(rule_data["id"])` reuses the original UUID from the snapshot. This means:
   - **Good**: External references (analytics, audit logs) remain valid
   - **Risk**: If a rule was deleted and a new one created with a different UUID, rollback would recreate the old UUID, potentially conflicting with the new one. The explicit DELETE before INSERT prevents this.

4. **Diff doesn't compare condition modifications** (lines 398-403): Conditions are only checked for added/removed, not modified. If a condition's `operator` or `condition_value` changes between versions, the diff won't show it. Rules have field-level diff via `_diff_rule`, but conditions don't have an equivalent `_diff_condition`.

5. **`_build_snapshot` includes `playbook.rules` JSONB** (line 134): This captures the legacy `Playbook.rules` JSONB field in the snapshot, even though it's not used. This is harmless but adds to snapshot size.

### Verdict: Well-engineered, production-ready versioning
- Schema migration won't break versioning — it's schema-agnostic
- Rollback is properly atomic via transaction
- Missing: version retention policy, condition-level diff
- No regex or AI dependency — purely structural

## Iteration 33 — OOXML-1: redline_implementer.py Full Audit (525 lines)
**Task:** Document EXACTLY how OOXML is generated.
**File:** `backend/app/services/redline_implementer.py` (525 lines)

### Architecture Overview
3-step pipeline: Find Anchor → Compute Diff → Generate OOXML

**Step 1: `find_anchor()` (lines 74-139) — Multi-strategy text search**
1. **Hash match**: SHA-256 lookup in ContractMap nodes (from structure_extractor.py)
2. **Exact match**: `full_text.find(target_text)` — simple substring
3. **Fuzzy match**: rapidfuzz Levenshtein against ContractMap nodes, then sliding window fallback

**Step 2: `_compute_word_ops()` (lines 256-282) — Word-level diff**
- Splits on whitespace using `re.split(r'(\s+)', text)` preserving whitespace tokens
- Uses `SequenceMatcher` to compute opcodes: equal/delete/insert/replace
- Returns list of `('keep'|'del'|'ins', text)` operations
- If <15% overlap → falls back to bulk replace (line 334)

**Step 3: `generate_track_changes_ooxml()` (lines 308-364) — OOXML generation**
- For each word-level op:
  - `keep` → `<w:r><w:t>text</w:t></w:r>` (normal run)
  - `del` → `<w:del><w:r><w:delText>text</w:delText></w:r></w:del>` (red strikethrough)
  - `ins` → `<w:ins><w:r><w:t>text</w:t></w:r></w:ins>` (green underline)
- Wrapped in full OOXML package (lines 284-306)

### Critical Findings

**1. The OOXML itself IS word-level diffs — NOT bulk replacement**
The PRD concern that "the OOXML generation replaces whole paragraphs" is INCORRECT for the backend. The backend generates surgical word-level diffs:
- `SequenceMatcher` computes word-by-word operations
- Only changed words get `<w:del>` / `<w:ins>` markup
- Unchanged words appear as normal `<w:r>` runs
- **The bug is in the FRONTEND**, not the backend OOXML generation

**2. Sliding window fuzzy search is O(n²) (lines 185-244)**
For a target of length T in document of length D:
- Slides window of size T through document with step T/4
- Then checks adjusted windows around the best match
- For T=200 chars in D=100,000 chars: ~2,000 fuzz.ratio comparisons
- Each `fuzz.ratio` call is O(T²) → total: O(D × T) which is manageable
- But for multiple redlines (20+), this adds up

**3. OOXML package structure is minimal (lines 284-306)**
Only includes:
- `/_rels/.rels` — required relationship
- `/word/document.xml` — content with `<w:body><w:p>...</w:p></w:body>`
Missing:
- No `<w:styles>` — inherits styles from host document ✓ (correct for insertOoxml)
- No `<w:numbering>` — numbered lists would lose numbering
- No `<w:rPr>` for font/size — assumes host document formatting. If the host paragraph uses a different font, the inserted OOXML won't match.

**4. Bulk fallback uses hardcoded 15% overlap threshold (line 334)**
If less than 15% of word-ops are `keep`, the entire text is replaced as bulk (full red → full green). This threshold is reasonable but not configurable.

**5. `_escape_xml` is duplicated (lines 246-254 and 398-404)**
Two identical escape functions. Minor code smell.

**6. `generate_insert_only_ooxml` has leading space (line 429)**
`<w:t xml:space="preserve"> {new_escaped}</w:t>` — note the space before `{new_escaped}`. This adds an unwanted leading space to inserted clauses.

**7. XML special characters in author name (line 350)**
`w:author="{author}"` — if author contains `"` or `<`, the XML would be malformed. Should escape the author name. Currently hardcoded as "ContraRed AI" which is safe, but the parameter accepts arbitrary strings.

### Key Insight for OOXML-4 (Root Cause)
The backend generates CORRECT word-level OOXML. The "whole paragraph turns red" bug must originate from:
1. The frontend's `findTextInDocument()` returning a paragraph-level range
2. `insertOoxml(..., replace)` replacing the entire range (which is paragraph-sized)
3. Even though the OOXML has word-level diffs, Word treats the entire replaced range as revised

The fix is in the frontend range precision, not the backend OOXML.

## Iteration 34 — OOXML-2: findTextInDocument() Audit (lines 884-963 of taskpane.ts)
**Task:** Document the 3-tier search strategy and range precision.

### 3-Tier Search Strategy

**Tier 1: Exact match via `body.search()` (lines 891-901)**
- Word API caps search text at 255 characters
- If text > 255 chars: truncates to first 255 and searches
- Returns first match (items[0])
- Confidence: 1.0 (exact) or 0.9 (truncated)
- **Range precision: TIGHT** — `body.search()` returns a Range of exactly the found text

**Tier 2: Normalized match (lines 903-913)**
- Strips ALL punctuation: `searchText.replace(/[^\w\s]/g, '')`
- Truncates to first 150 chars
- Searches with matchCase: false
- Confidence: 0.8
- **Range precision: MODERATE** — normalized text may match a substring within a paragraph

**Tier 3: Fuzzy match via Fuse.js on paragraphs (lines 915-960)**
- Loads ALL paragraphs from document
- Fuse.js threshold: 0.25 (75% similarity minimum)
- Minimum fuzzy confidence: 0.6
- **Range precision: PARAGRAPH-LEVEL** — `matchedParagraph.paragraph.getRange()` returns the ENTIRE paragraph range (line 955)
- **THIS IS THE ROOT CAUSE OF "WHOLE PARAGRAPH TURNS RED"**

### Range Precision Analysis by Tier

| Tier | Search Method | Range Returned | Precision |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 (Exact) | `body.search(text)` | Exact text range | TIGHT ✓ |
| 2 (Normalized) | `body.search(normalized)` | Exact text range | TIGHT ✓ |
| 3 (Fuzzy) | `paragraph.getRange()` | Full paragraph | PARAGRAPH ✗ |

### Critical Findings

**1. Tier 3 fuzzy returns PARAGRAPH range — confirmed root cause (line 955)**
```typescript
const range = matchedParagraph.paragraph.getRange();
```
When Tier 3 activates, the returned range is the entire paragraph. When `insertOoxml(ooxml, Word.InsertLocation.replace)` is called on this range (in `applyAIRedline()`), it replaces the ENTIRE paragraph with the OOXML. Even though the OOXML has word-level diffs, Word treats the whole operation as a revision.

**2. Tier 1 truncation at 255 chars causes partial matching (line 893-894)**
```typescript
const tier1Text = searchText.length <= WORD_SEARCH_LIMIT
    ? searchText
    : searchText.slice(0, WORD_SEARCH_LIMIT);
```
If the target text is 500 chars and the first 255 match a different clause, Tier 1 returns the WRONG range. Confidence is 0.9 (not 1.0) but this is still treated as a valid match.

**3. Tier 2 strips ALL punctuation (line 904)**
```typescript
const normalizedSearch = searchText.replace(/[^\w\s]/g, '').trim().slice(0, 150);
```
This removes periods, commas, semicolons, quotes, parentheses. "Section 8.2(a)" becomes "Section 82a". This could match different text. Also, for legal text heavy with punctuation, the normalized version may be too generic.

**4. Tier 1 always takes first match (line 900)**
```typescript
return { range: exactResults.items[0], ... };
```
If the same text appears multiple times in the document (common: "shall indemnify and hold harmless"), the FIRST occurrence is always selected. This could be the wrong instance.

**5. Fuse.js threshold 0.25 = 75% minimum (line 932)**
Combined with MIN_FUZZY_CONFIDENCE of 0.6, effectively needs 60% confidence to accept. This is quite lenient — a paragraph sharing only 60% words with the search text could match. In legal documents with similar boilerplate paragraphs, false matches are likely.

**6. No search prioritization for multiple Tier 1 matches**
If `body.search()` returns multiple matches (items.length > 1), only the first is used. The code should prioritize by:
- Document position (nearest to the risk's known position)
- Surrounding context
- Exact vs partial match

### Summary
- **Tier 1 and 2**: Range is tight (exact search text match) ✓
- **Tier 3**: Range is paragraph-level — THIS is the "whole paragraph turns red" bug
- **Prevalence**: Tier 3 activates when AI quotes don't match document text exactly (common with LLMs)
- **Fix direction**: Tier 3 should `paragraph.search()` within the matched paragraph to get a tighter range

## Iteration 35 — OOXML-3: applyAIRedline() Audit (lines 2329-2428 of taskpane.ts)
**Task:** Document EXACTLY how OOXML gets inserted into Word. What is the range at insertion time?

### Flow
1. **Pre-fetch OOXML** (line 2348): Calls `api.generateRedlineZDR()` BEFORE entering `Word.run` to avoid context timeout. This is smart — the API call can take seconds, and Word contexts timeout.
2. **Enable Track Changes** (line 2360): Sets `changeTrackingMode = Word.ChangeTrackingMode.trackAll`. Wrapped in try/catch — Office webview may not support this.
3. **Find text** (line 2367): `findTextInDocument(redline.original_text, context)` — the 3-tier search from OOXML-2.
4. **Insert OOXML** (lines 2375-2379):
   - Missing clauses: `range.insertOoxml(ooxml, Word.InsertLocation.after)` — appends after range
   - Violations: `range.insertOoxml(ooxml, Word.InsertLocation.replace)` — **REPLACES the entire range**
5. **Insert comment** (line 2384): Adds explanation as Word comment on the range
6. **Store undo info** (lines 2392-2413): Saves paragraph index + context hash for undo targeting

### THE KEY LINE — Root Cause Confirmed

```typescript
result.range.insertOoxml(redlineResponse.ooxml, Word.InsertLocation.replace);  // line 2378
```

**What `result.range` is at this point:**
- If Tier 1 or 2 found the text: Range is the EXACT matching text → OOXML replaces only that text ✓
- If Tier 3 (fuzzy) found the text: Range is the ENTIRE PARAGRAPH → OOXML replaces the ENTIRE paragraph ✗

**The OOXML contains word-level diffs** (from backend `generate_track_changes_ooxml()`), but when `insertOoxml(..., replace)` replaces a paragraph-level range:
1. Word deletes the entire paragraph content
2. Word inserts the OOXML content (which includes keep/del/ins runs)
3. If Track Changes is ON: the delete of the paragraph + insert of OOXML are BOTH tracked
4. Result: The whole paragraph appears as a revision (red strikethrough + green underline)

### Critical Findings

**1. Double-tracking problem with Track Changes ON**
When `changeTrackingMode = trackAll` is set (line 2360) AND `insertOoxml(..., replace)` is used:
- Track Changes records the REPLACEMENT operation itself as a change
- The OOXML already contains its OWN `<w:del>` and `<w:ins>` markup
- Result: **DOUBLE revision marks** — Word's track changes wrapping the OOXML's track changes
- This is the fundamental incompatibility: you can't use OOXML-embedded track changes AND Word's live Track Changes simultaneously

**2. Missing clause handling is correct (line 2376)**
`InsertLocation.after` appends the OOXML after the found range without replacing anything. This works correctly because there's no existing text to match.

**3. Fix text guard is good (lines 2330-2335)**
Prevents falling back to recommendation text as replacement. Without this guard, the recommendation (e.g., "Consider capping liability at 12 months") would be inserted as literal contract text.

**4. Read-only document check (lines 2337-2342)**
Calls `isDocumentReadOnly()` before attempting changes. Good defensive programming.

**5. Undo info storage uses substring matching (line 2399)**
```typescript
allParas.items[pi].text.includes(textToApply.substring(0, 50))
```
After applying the fix, searches paragraphs for the first 50 chars of the fix text to find its location. If the fix text appears in multiple paragraphs, wrong location stored.

**6. Comment insertion may fail silently (lines 2383-2389)**
Comments require WordApi 1.4 — graceful fallback is correct, but the user doesn't know the comment wasn't added.

### Root Cause Summary
The "whole paragraph turns red" bug has TWO contributing causes:
1. **Tier 3 fuzzy** returns paragraph-level range → `replace` replaces entire paragraph
2. **Track Changes double-tracking** — Word's live Track Changes wraps the OOXML's embedded track changes, creating double revision marks even for Tier 1/2 matches

## Iteration 36 — OOXML-4: Root Cause Analysis — "Whole Paragraph Turns Red"
**Task:** Trace the exact failure path. Synthesize OOXML-1, OOXML-2, OOXML-3 findings.

### THE EXACT FAILURE PATH

```
Step 1: AI generates a fix for "The Vendor shall bear unlimited liability"
        → Backend produces OOXML with word-level diff:
          <w:r><w:t>The Vendor shall bear </w:t></w:r>
          <w:del><w:delText>unlimited</w:delText></w:del>
          <w:ins><w:t>liability capped at 12 months of fees</w:t></w:ins>
        → This OOXML is CORRECT — only "unlimited" is struck, replacement inserted

Step 2: Frontend calls findTextInDocument("The Vendor shall bear unlimited liability")
        → Tier 1 (body.search): May fail if AI quoted text doesn't exactly match
          (LLMs commonly add/remove minor words, change punctuation)
        → Tier 2 (normalized): May fail if normalization removes too much
        → Tier 3 (Fuse.js fuzzy): MATCHES a paragraph that contains this text
          → Returns: paragraph.getRange() — the ENTIRE paragraph

Step 3: The paragraph is 400 chars long:
        "8.1 Liability. The Vendor shall bear unlimited liability for any and
        all losses, damages, claims, and expenses arising from this Agreement,
        including but not limited to direct, indirect, consequential, and
        special damages of any kind whatsoever."
        → The returned Range spans ALL 400 characters

Step 4: applyAIRedline() calls:
        range.insertOoxml(ooxml, Word.InsertLocation.replace)
        → Word REPLACES all 400 characters with the OOXML content
        → The OOXML only contains ~60 characters of content (the diff around "unlimited")

Step 5: Result in Word:
        → The entire 400-char paragraph is DELETED (red strikethrough)
        → The 60-char OOXML content is INSERTED (green underline)
        → User sees: entire paragraph struck through, replaced with a fragment
        → THIS IS THE BUG
```

### ROOT CAUSE #1: Tier 3 Range is Paragraph-Level (PRIMARY)
**File:** `ContraRed-PoC/src/taskpane/taskpane.ts`, line 955
```typescript
const range = matchedParagraph.paragraph.getRange();
```
When Fuse.js fuzzy matching activates (Tier 3), it returns the range of the entire matched paragraph, not the specific clause within it. The OOXML replacement then replaces ALL text in that paragraph with only the diffed portion.

**When does Tier 3 activate?**
- When AI quotes text that doesn't exactly match the document (common with LLMs)
- When text is >255 chars (Tier 1 truncates, may fail to match)
- When text contains special punctuation that normalization strips (Tier 2 fails)
- **Estimated frequency: 30-50% of all fix applications** based on LLM quoting accuracy

### ROOT CAUSE #2: OOXML Contains Only the Diff, Not the Full Paragraph (SECONDARY)
**File:** `backend/app/services/redline_implementer.py`, line 482
```python
ooxml = self.generate_track_changes_ooxml(
    original=anchor.text,  # This is the AI's quoted text, NOT the full paragraph
    replacement=suggested_text
)
```
The OOXML is generated from the AI's quoted `original_text` (e.g., 60 chars) vs the `suggested_text`. It does NOT include the rest of the paragraph. So when this OOXML replaces a 400-char paragraph range, the 340 non-diffed characters simply vanish.

### ROOT CAUSE #3: Track Changes Double-Tracking (TERTIARY)
**File:** `ContraRed-PoC/src/taskpane/taskpane.ts`, line 2360
```typescript
context.document.changeTrackingMode = Word.ChangeTrackingMode.trackAll;
```
Even when Tier 1/2 returns a tight range, Track Changes is enabled BEFORE the OOXML insertion. This means:
- Word tracks the `insertOoxml(replace)` operation as a change
- The OOXML already contains `<w:del>` and `<w:ins>` markup
- Result: double-tracked revision marks (Word's tracking + OOXML's tracking)

### PRECISE ROOT CAUSE STATEMENT
**The bug occurs because `findTextInDocument()` Tier 3 returns a paragraph-level range, but `generate_track_changes_ooxml()` produces OOXML covering only the AI's quoted text fragment. When `insertOoxml(replace)` replaces the large range with the small OOXML, the entire paragraph content outside the diff is destroyed.**

### Why This Wasn't Caught Earlier
- When Tier 1 or 2 matches (exact search hit), the range IS tight and the replacement works correctly
- Developers likely tested with short, exact-match clauses
- The bug only manifests with longer clauses, fuzzy matches, or AI paraphrasing — which are common in production but rare in testing

### Severity: P0
- **Data loss**: Paragraph content outside the diff is permanently deleted
- **Trust destruction**: A lawyer seeing their carefully drafted clause destroyed would immediately distrust the tool
- **Frequency**: 30-50% of fix applications (any time Tier 3 activates)
- **Undo exists** but requires the user to notice the damage and click Undo

## Iteration 37 — OOXML-5: body.search() 255-Character Limit Analysis
**Task:** Document the 255-char limit, what happens with long clauses, and how it interacts with range precision.

### The 255-Character Limit
**File:** `ContraRed-PoC/src/taskpane/taskpane.ts`, line 889
```typescript
const WORD_SEARCH_LIMIT = 255;
```

The Word JavaScript API `body.search()` method has a hard 255-character limit on the search string. This is a Microsoft API constraint, not a ContraRed limitation.

### Current Handling (lines 892-894)
```typescript
const tier1Text = searchText.length <= WORD_SEARCH_LIMIT
    ? searchText
    : searchText.slice(0, WORD_SEARCH_LIMIT);
```
For text longer than 255 chars, the code truncates to the FIRST 255 characters and searches for that substring.

### What Happens with Long Clauses

**Scenario 1: Unique first 255 chars (WORKS)**
Clause: "The Vendor hereby agrees to indemnify, defend, and hold harmless the Client..." (500 chars)
→ First 255 chars are searched
→ If unique in the document, Tier 1 finds the right location
→ BUT: the returned range covers only the first 255 chars, NOT the full 500-char clause
→ `insertOoxml(replace)` replaces only those 255 chars
→ The remaining 245 chars of the clause remain in the document UNCHANGED
→ **Result: partial replacement — first half replaced, second half orphaned**

**Scenario 2: Non-unique first 255 chars (WRONG MATCH)**
In an agreement with multiple indemnification clauses, each starting with similar boilerplate:
→ "The Vendor hereby agrees to indemnify..." appears in Section 5.1 AND Section 8.3
→ Tier 1 returns `items[0]` — the FIRST occurrence (line 900)
→ If the AI flagged Section 8.3, the fix gets applied to Section 5.1
→ **Result: wrong clause modified — potentially changing a clause that was correct**

**Scenario 3: First 255 chars don't match (FALLTHROUGH)**
AI quoted text has minor differences in the first 255 chars:
→ Tier 1 fails → Tier 2 tries normalized (first 150 chars)
→ Tier 2 also fails → Tier 3 (Fuse.js fuzzy) on paragraphs
→ Falls to paragraph-level range → ROOT CAUSE #1 from OOXML-4
→ **Result: entire paragraph replaced**

### Interaction with Range Precision

| Clause Length | Tier 1 Behavior | Range Covers | Problem |
|---|---|---|---|
| ≤255 chars | Full text search | Full clause | ✓ No issue |
| 256-510 chars | First 255 chars | First 255 chars only | Partial replacement — second half orphaned |
| >510 chars | First 255 chars | First 255 chars only | Large orphaned tail + possible wrong match |
| Any length, no match | Falls to Tier 3 | Entire paragraph | Paragraph destroyed (OOXML-4) |

### How Common Are Long Clauses?

Enterprise contracts routinely have clauses exceeding 255 characters:
- **Indemnification clauses**: 400-1500 chars (multi-party, carve-outs, procedures)
- **Limitation of liability**: 300-800 chars (caps, exclusions, super-caps)
- **Confidentiality definitions**: 500-2000 chars (categories, exceptions, marking requirements)
- **Force majeure**: 400-1200 chars (event lists, notification, termination triggers)
- **Data protection**: 500-1500 chars (GDPR/DPDP compliance, processor obligations)

**Estimated impact**: 40-60% of flagged clauses in enterprise contracts exceed 255 chars.

### Critical Findings

**1. Partial replacement creates orphaned text (PRIMARY)**
When Tier 1 matches the first 255 chars of a 500-char clause, `insertOoxml(replace)` only replaces the first 255 chars. The remaining 245 chars stay in the document, creating a grammatically broken paragraph.

**2. No verification that the full text matches (line 900)**
Confidence is set to 0.9 for truncated matches, but there's no check that the characters AFTER position 255 also match. The code assumes the first 255 chars are sufficient to uniquely identify the clause.

**3. Tier 2 truncates even further to 150 chars (line 904)**
```typescript
const normalizedSearch = searchText.replace(/[^\w\s]/g, '').trim().slice(0, 150);
```
This reduces search precision even more, increasing wrong-match risk.

### Proposed Mitigations
1. **Split-and-verify**: Search first 255 chars, then verify the found range extends to contain the remaining text by loading and comparing
2. **Paragraph-then-narrow**: First identify which paragraph contains the clause, then use `paragraph.search()` for the specific sub-phrase being changed
3. **Use `range.expandTo()`**: After finding the first 255 chars, expand the range to encompass the full clause text by checking adjacent content
4. **Multiple search passes**: Search for the first 255 chars, then search for chars 200-455, then verify both ranges are in the same paragraph
